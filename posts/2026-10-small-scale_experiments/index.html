TL;DR

We find that scaling laws hold all the way down to 4M parameters, but small models follow them only when each model is tuned close to the best loss its hyperparameters can reach. In fact, we demonstrate that small models are much more sensitive to their hyperparameters than large ones, so tuning can take hundreds of configurations per model size at small scales. The noisy quadratic, a quadratic description of the loss near the best hyperparameters, models this tuning noise well and tells us how many runs each scale needs. The small scale sensitivity to hyperparameters is not a big limitation in practice, since small runs are cheap. Therefore, the level of rigor required for proper scaling is affordable, and we show how to split a compute budget across model sizes to make the extrapolation as accurate as possible. Figure 1 summarizes these findings. Finally, we recommend the following to practitioners: tune thoroughly at small scales, use fewer runs at large scales, check each assumption with simple diagnostics, and compare models where scaling laws are most reliable.

What are scaling laws, and what do we use them for?

Training a large language model can cost millions of dollars, so before launching the final run, we want to be confident in every choice that goes into it, from the architecture and the optimizer to the learning rate, the batch size, and how long to train. A wrong choice at that scale can waste much of the budget, and we cannot afford to try the alternatives at full size. Scaling laws are how we make these choices without paying for full-size trials. We train many small models, measure how their loss improves as they grow, and extrapolate to the size we care about.

Most of what we know about building language models comes from experiments, and experiments have become expensive. The cost of training the most compute-intensive models has grown by about 2.4 times per year since 2016 (Cottier et al. [1]), and most of the compute that goes into developing a model is spent on experiments, with only a minority going to the final training run (Denain and Wu [2]). In parallel, the science of scaling has been evolving and dictates that instead of tuning hyperparameters on big models, we can train many small models, learn from them, and extrapolate, so that we get the big model right in one go (Hashimoto [3], Lecture 9). Scaling laws are the tool that makes this extrapolation possible.

A scaling law is an empirical formula that predicts the loss of a model from the resources used to train it. The most common form, popularized by Hoffmann et al. [4] and building on Rosenfeld et al. [5], writes the pretraining loss as a function of the number of parameters p and the number of training tokens d,

\[\mathcal{L}(p, d) = \epsilon + \frac{\zeta}{p^{\iota}} + \frac{\eta}{d^{\kappa}}\]

The constant ε is the irreducible loss, the loss that even an infinitely large model trained on infinite data would pay because text is inherently unpredictable. The other two terms are the excess loss from having finitely many parameters and from training on finitely many tokens. The constants ζ and η set the size of these two terms, and the exponents ι and κ set how fast each term shrinks as we add parameters and tokens. We fit all five constants, ε, ζ, η, ι, and κ, to the losses of small models and use the fitted formula to predict the loss of larger ones. Compute grows with the product of parameters and tokens, so for a fixed budget the formula also tells us how to trade parameters against data. On a log-log plot, the loss looks like a straight line until it starts to bend toward ε.

The idea of scaling laws predates large language models, since Cortes et al. [6] already fit learning curves of this shape on small runs and extrapolated them (Hashimoto [3], Lecture 9). Hestness et al. [7] and Kaplan et al. [8] then found power laws across domains and over many orders of magnitude, and Hoffmann et al. [4] trained over 400 models to conclude that parameters and tokens should grow at the same rate, about 20 tokens per parameter.

Scaling laws achieve three objectives:

  1. They predict the loss of a large run before we pay for it, as OpenAI did for some aspects of GPT-4 using models trained with at most one thousandth of its compute (OpenAI [9]). The left panel of Figure 2 sketches this use.

  2. They let us compare designs by how they scale. Kaplan et al. [8] compared LSTMs with transformers by training both at many compute levels and found a constant gap, with LSTMs needing many times more compute for the same loss, an answer that would otherwise have required training a very large LSTM (Hashimoto [3], Lecture 9). The middle panel of Figure 2 sketches such a comparison, where family B reaches every loss with less compute than family A.

  3. They tell us how to spend a given compute budget. For each budget, we train models of several sizes and find the size with the lowest loss, which is how Llama 3 chose its model size (Grattafiori et al. [10]). The right panel of Figure 2 shows how the best model size grows with the budget.

Scaling laws also guide and depend on hyperparameters, since the best learning rate and batch size shift as models grow (Hashimoto [3], Lecture 9), so some teams fit power laws to their best values (DeepSeek-AI [11], Li et al. [12], Bergsma et al. [13]). Others use a parametrization such as muP, which rescales the initialization and the learning rate of each layer with the width so that the best learning rate stays roughly fixed, and tune once on a small model (Yang et al. [14], Hashimoto [3], Lecture 9). Downstream accuracy is harder to predict, since scaling laws are well behaved for the pretraining loss but much less so for benchmark accuracy (Hashimoto [3], Lecture 9), and a recent meta-analysis found that only 39% of downstream scaling laws were predictable (Lourie, Hu, and Cho [15]).

In summary, the design procedure for scaling usually implies training a few small models spanning a couple of orders of magnitude in compute, checking that the loss follows a clean law, and using the law to set hyperparameters and choose architectures for the large run (Hashimoto [3], Lecture 9). If this worked as advertised, most of the cost of model development would disappear since it would move to smaller scales that cost significantly less than larger runs, as Figure 1(c) shows. In the remainder of this post, we explain why scaling laws might fail to deliver on this promise and how to remedy such failure.

So what do you do if your scaling law is off and what could have caused this failure?

The two most influential scaling studies on scaling laws already disagree. Kaplan et al. [8] found that the best model size grows with compute to the power 0.73, while Hoffmann et al. [4] found 0.50, and follow-up work traced the gap to choices that look like details, such as how to count parameters, how long to warm up, whether to retune the optimizer at each scale, and cosine schedules that cannot be cut short (Porian et al. [16], Pearce and Song [17], Hashimoto [3], Lecture 9). A replication of one of Chinchilla's fits traced its disagreement with the other methods to the curve fitting rather than the data (Besiroglu et al. [18]), and a survey of more than 50 scaling studies found laws built from models with 100M parameters or fewer unreliable (Li, Kudugunta, and Zettlemoyer [19]). Li et al. [20] call this the scaling gap, where insights from small-scale experiments fail to transfer to larger scales, and many concluded that scaling laws only become reliable at large scales.

We argue the opposite in this work and show that scaling laws hold for models as small as 4M parameters, which train in under an hour on a single GPU. Earlier work did not reach this conclusion because small models are much more sensitive to their hyperparameters, which makes them much harder to tune.

Scaling laws describe the fully tuned frontier

What we mean by the fully tuned frontier. For a given number of parameters and training tokens, the fully tuned frontier is the best loss that any hyperparameter configuration can reach. A scaling law describes this frontier, whereas a typical training run results in a loss somewhere above it, and how far above depends on how thorough the tuning was.

Our setup. We train Llama-style models with Meta Lingua [21] on the 100B-token subset of FineWeb-Edu [22], at target sizes that double from 4M to 268M effective parameters, counting parameters according to their contribution to compute (Porian et al. [16]). The tokenizer and vocabulary are fixed, and the context length is 1,024 tokens.

Our initial model-ladder sweep sampled 128 configurations of seven training hyperparameters per size, or 64 at 268M, from the same ranges at every size. Figures 1 and 6 use this initial sweep. We later expanded the searches at the four smallest scales: the final ladder has 867, 826, 867, and 934 completed configurations at 4M, 8M, 17M, and 34M, respectively, excluding two failed runs at 4M. It has 128 configurations at 67M and 134M and 64 at 268M. A separate experiment also samples the number of layers and attention heads.

Each main run trains on 32 tokens per target parameter. We branch a learning-rate decay from eight checkpoints and extend each by 25%, giving eight training lengths from 5 to 40 tokens per parameter. We fit laws to the best loss at each parameter–data budget, dropping the two shortest lengths and leaving 24 fitting points at 4M–34M. We use 67M and 134M for validation and 268M for testing. The 4M–134M models train on one A100 GPU each; the 268M models use eight.

Figure 3 shows every run in our search, and its dotted line marks the frontier, which different runs reach at different compute budgets. To fit the law, we take the lowest validation loss, in bits per character, over all configurations at each model size and training length, so different points can come from different configurations. Like prior work (Hilton et al. [23], Li, Kudugunta, and Zettlemoyer [19]), we found that the shortest runs fit poorly, so we drop the lengths of 5 and 10 tokens per parameter and fit the remaining 24 points, from 15 to 40 tokens per parameter at the four fitting sizes. In our ablations, taking the best configuration for each training length instead of the best run for each size halved the test error, decaying the learning rate cut it by 98%, and six ways of counting parameters all gave reasonable laws, although their test errors still differ by more than two orders of magnitude.

In Figure 4, we show how the amount of tuning determines how well the law fits and extrapolates. In its leftmost panel, with the best of 4 random configurations per size, the losses scatter around the fit, and the law misses the held-out 268M model with a test mean squared error of 1.3e-2. The test error is still 6.0e-3 with 16 configurations in the second panel and 2.8e-3 with 64 in the third, and with 256 in the rightmost panel it falls to 3.7e-6, more than 3,000 times smaller than with 4. Figure 1(b) shows the same effect on a single prediction, where the law fit to the best of 4 configurations predicts 0.63 bits per character for the 268M model, which reaches 0.76, while the law fit to all 128 predicts 0.77. These numbers depend on the search space, which Appendix B of our paper [24] lists in full. We believe a good range is one where the best configuration lands inside it rather than at its edge, and we kept this consideration in mind as we designed the search space.

Distinguishing our contributions from common wisdom and existing work. That small models need careful tuning is not news to practitioners (Ivgi et al. [25]). What we contribute in this work is an understanding of how much more tuning they need and why, how to check that a search has gone far enough, and how to split a budget across sizes. Hyperparameter under-tuning has also been shown in the literature to result in misleading conclusions. For instance, Xiao [26] concluded that small batch sizes train language models poorly, and Marek et al. [27] showed in their Figure 5 that this came from leaving Adam's β2 untuned for the small batches.

Small models are harder to tune

We demonstrate that small models need this much tuning because they are more sensitive to their hyperparameters. This may seem counterintuitive, since large runs are the ones we hear about failing, but both observations do not contradict each other. Instabilities do appear as training scales up especially beyond the sizes we cover here, and easier tuning does not make large-scale training simple. What we quantify here is that under the same search space, the share of configurations that reach a near-optimal loss grows with model size, so we find that below roughly 67M parameters tuning has to be intensive.

Random search quantifies this notion of sensitivity, since the share of uniformly sampled configurations that land near the best loss tells us how much of the space is good. Figure 1(a) shows this share at two sizes. At 4M parameters, only 10 out of the 126 configurations that finished training, about 8%, land within 0.1 bits per character of the best one, while at 134M, 36 out of 128 do, about 28%. Figure 5 shows the full distribution of losses at 4M, 17M, 67M, and 268M. From left to right, it goes from broad and flat at 4M to a narrow peak next to the best loss at 268M.

Takeaway 1. Small-scale experiments are more sensitive to tuning.

Under the same search space, far fewer configurations land near the best loss at small scales, so small models need many more tuning runs before their best losses follow the scaling law.

To see why this is the case, consider the loss as a function of the hyperparameters. Near the optimum it looks like a bowl with a few steep directions and many nearly flat ones, blurred a little by noise from random seeds, a model that Lourie et al. [28] call the noisy quadratic and show to fit well. With one steep direction, a random configuration only needs to land near the optimum along that direction, whereas with two it must do so along both at once, so good configurations become much rarer.

What we mean by the effective number of hyperparameters. It is the number of steep directions in the bowl around the optimum, written γ. We estimate it by fitting the noisy quadratic to the best losses from random search. Because it describes the loss surface near the optimum, it depends much less on how wide the search space is than the share of good configurations does.

Fitting the noisy quadratic at every size, we find that out of the seven to nine hyperparameters we tune, small models have two effective hyperparameters, while models with 67M parameters or more have one. The drop comes mostly from adding parameters rather than data, and why it happens is still open, since earlier work found the same effective number across architectures that share a search space (Lourie, He, and Cho [28]), and a parametrization such as muP may change it. Figure 6 shows the consequence in the same format as Figure 1(a), for every size from 4M to 134M, and its bar chart counts the configurations inside the 0.1 BPC circle at each size. Figure 7 shows the effective number of hyperparameters at every model size and training length.

[28]
N. Lourie, H. He, and K. Cho, “Hyperparameter loss surfaces are simple near their optima,” in COLM, 2025.

In our paper, we show that the expected gap between the best of M random configurations and the optimum shrinks like \(M^{-2/\gamma}\), so if 100 runs reach a given gap when γ = 2, about 14 reach it when γ = 1, while γ = 3 would need about 860. This is how much more tuning small models need, assuming the noise from random seeds is small.

Takeaway 2. We can model the random search with the noisy quadratic.

The noisy quadratic describes how the best losses from random search are distributed, which lets us measure the effective number of hyperparameters at each scale and predict how many runs each scale needs.

A model of the loss surface that spans scales is hard to find

We also tried to combine the noisy quadratic, which describes the loss surface at each scale, with the scaling law, which describes its minimum across scales, into one model that predicts the best hyperparameters at larger scales. None of the variants we tried comes close to the scaling law. On the held-out 268M model, the best one has a mean squared error of 0.0066 over all configurations, vs. 0.00002 for the scaling law at the best hyperparameters, so we tune each scale separately.

Takeaway 3. A hyperparameter model that spans scales is hard to find.

Simple models of the loss surface capture its coarse shape across scales but not its finer details, so we tune each scale on its own instead of predicting its hyperparameters from smaller ones.

Extrapolation hits statistical limits

Even a well-tuned law runs into a second problem far from the data. We repeated the fit with independent samples of 128 random configurations per scale, and Figure 8 shows that the estimates agree well near the data but spread apart far from it, with irreducible losses from about 0.42 to 0.67 bits per character. Far out, the prediction is dominated by the irreducible loss ε, which is hard to pin down while the curve still looks like a straight line on a log-log plot, so small differences in the measured losses move the extrapolation a lot.

This limitation matters when comparing models, since if we extrapolate two laws far enough, we are mostly comparing their estimates of ε. Our laws extrapolate well about an order of magnitude beyond the fitted range, from 34M to 268M parameters, and further out the prediction leans more and more on ε.

So what do we recommend for optimal scaling?

Our recommendations rest on three facts:

  1. With the pretraining data fixed, a model's capabilities depend on its pretraining loss.

  2. With thorough tuning, pretraining scaling laws hold down to tiny models.

  3. And as models grow, they become much less sensitive to their hyperparameters, so the final scale-up needs far less tuning, which may also explain why hyperparameter transfer works reasonably well.

These facts support model-centric research, where the data stays fixed and we change the model. Data-centric research needs different tools, because changing the data breaks the link between loss and capabilities.

Hold the data fixed and compare models by pretraining loss. Better loss does not always mean better downstream accuracy (McKenzie et al. [29], Wei et al. [30]), but we only need models with the same loss to have the same capabilities, which Mayilvahanan et al. [31] showed holds when the pretraining data is fixed, a property we call the perplexity-capability correspondence. A family with a better scaling law then reaches every loss, and every capability, at a lower cost.

Tune thoroughly where tuning is cheap, and verify that the search converged. For a fixed ratio of tokens to parameters, compute is quadratic in model size, so a model one tenth the size costs one hundredth as much, and we can afford far larger sweeps at small scale. In our experiments, one 268M run costs as much as 4 runs at 134M, 67 at 34M, or about 5,000 at 4M, so what we should budget is the total compute, sweeps included. While we use uniform random search here to measure the loss surface and run these diagnostics, for practical tuning we recommend Bayesian optimization, such as Hyperopt or Vizier, or a manual multi-step grid. If the best losses do not match the noisy quadratic, the search has probably not found the optimum, and our opda library implements this verification.

Use hyperparameter transfer, but tune its starting point carefully. With muP, learning rates tuned on a small model transfer well across sizes, although muP can break with learnable gains, unusual optimizers such as Lion, or strong weight decay (Hashimoto [3], Lecture 11). Our results do not question whether transfer works, but whether the small-scale sweep that transfer starts from finds the optimum in the first place. Transfer moves all the tuning to the smallest scale, which is exactly where the optimum is hardest to find, and the noisy quadratic can confirm that the base sweep reached it. If that sweep misses the optimum, or leaves a hyperparameter such as β2 untuned for the batch size, the transferred configuration starts off wrong, and we can falsely conclude that transfer, or the idea being tested, did not work.

T. Hashimoto, “Scaling laws 1 and scaling laws 2 (CS336: Language modeling from scratch, spring 2025).” Stanford University, video lectures, 2025.

Spend the budget unevenly across scales. The goal is to minimize the error in the loss predicted at the target scale. A fit that looks accurate across the measured scales need not give the most accurate extrapolation.

For a pure power law, the fit is a linear regression in log-log space. Our paper uses this analysis to show how an error in a loss measurement shifts the target prediction through that point's leverage. Measurements near the ends of the fitted range generally have more influence than those near its center. We then tie the error at each scale to the tuning effort: with \(M_j\) random-search runs, the expected gap to the optimum shrinks like \(M_j^{-2/\gamma_j}\), where \(\gamma_j\) is the effective number of hyperparameters. Combining these two results with the cost of a run gives a closed-form allocation that minimizes a bound on the error at the target scale, under the assumptions in Appendix D [24].

Figure 9 follows the paper's four-scale example. A run costs four times as much at each successive scale, the first two scales have two effective hyperparameters and the last two have one, and the target is one step beyond the fitted range. The allocation uses 32, 1, 5, and 4 runs, with 8.6%, 1.1%, 21.6%, and 68.7% of the compute at the four scales. The smallest scale gets many cheap runs; the largest gets most of the budget even though it needs only a few runs. The near-central scale \(u_2\) has zero leverage in this example and is kept with one run to anchor the fit.

Appendix D gives the full derivation and the recipe for a chosen budget, per-run costs, and effective dimensions, treating the error amplitudes as comparable across scales. Testing experiments designed with this allocation is future work.

Takeaway 4. We can recommend how to spend compute for optimal extrapolation.

The paper combines each scale's leverage, tuning-error convergence, and per-run cost to choose the number of runs per scale for a fixed budget, under the assumptions of Appendix D.

Check if each assumption holds. We check our assumptions with four questions:

  • Do the best losses from random search match the noisy quadratic at each size?

  • Does tuning get easier as the model grows?

  • Do models with the same loss have the same capabilities on a few evaluations?

  • Does the law predict a held-out larger scale?

If a check fails, the likely cause is a bug, a modeling problem, or poor hyperparameters, so we fix the setup before judging the idea. When all four checks pass, we have good reason to expect the small-scale result to hold at larger scales.

Limitations and future work. Our findings apply to model-centric research, since changing the pretraining data breaks the perplexity-capability correspondence and downstream scaling laws are not reliable enough to replace it. Future work could develop reliable methods for predicting how changes to the data affect capabilities. Moreover, our results come from one corpus, FineWeb-Edu, with a fixed tokenizer and vocabulary, and our largest model has 268M parameters. Future work can investigate whether our findings hold across other datasets, tokenizers, vocabulary sizes, and larger models. Finally, experiments with parametrizations such as muP can test how they affect hyperparameter sensitivity and the tuning effort needed at each scale.
